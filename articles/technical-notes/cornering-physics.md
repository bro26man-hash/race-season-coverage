## 1. What Is G-Force?

**G-force** (gravitational force equivalent) is a **mass-specific force** — force per unit mass — expressed in units of standard gravity (symbol: **g** or **g₀**). It is *not* actually a force in the strict physics sense; it is a measure of **acceleration** that causes a perception of weight.

- **1 g** = standard gravitational acceleration at Earth's surface = **9.80665 m/s²** (≈ 35.3 km/h of velocity change per second).
- **0 g** = weightlessness. Gravitational acceleration alone does *not* produce g-force — objects in free fall experience zero g-force despite being under gravitational acceleration. G-force only arises from **mechanical contact forces** (seat, floor, track surface pushing back).
- **Negative g** = acceleration directed downward, producing a force that pulls the driver upward — potentially lifting them out of the seat.
- **Shock** = transient acceleration with significant jerk (rate of change of acceleration), measured in g₀·s and pulse duration.
- **Sign convention**: Positive g-force points downward (upward acceleration), producing compressive force pushing the driver into the seat. Negative g-force points upward.
- **Key distinction**: *g* (lowercase) is the unit; *G* (uppercase) is the gravitational constant. Don't confuse them in copy.

> **Key writing insight**: When a driver says they took a corner at "4 g", they mean the lateral acceleration was 4 × 9.81 m/s² — equivalent to four times their body weight pressing sideways into the seat.

## 2. Lateral G-Loads Through Corners

When a race car changes **direction** (not just speed), occupants experience **lateral (side-to-side) g-forces** generated by the mechanical push of the seat and tire contact with the track surface.

- Lateral g-force is the **centripetal acceleration** directed toward the center of the corner's arc.
- Per Newton's second law (*F = ma*), the lateral G-load is the centripetal force required to keep the car on its curved path, expressed as a fraction of standard gravity.
- **Formula**: lateral acceleration = **v² / r** (velocity squared divided by corner radius).
  - Doubling speed **quadruples** the lateral G-load
  - Tightening the radius (smaller *r*) increases it proportionally
- **Measured values in racing**:

| Scenario | Lateral G | Context |
|----------|-----------|---------|
| Gentle hairpin | 1–2 g | Low-speed technical corners (Monaco Loews) |
| Medium-speed B&M | 2–3 g | Typical medium corners (Spa Blanchimont) |
| High-speed corner | 3–5 g | Fast swept corners (Copse, COTA T1) |
| Dragster launch | **5.3 g** (horizontal) | Top fuel dragster acceleration (Wikipedia) |
| Extreme racing | Up to **6+ g** | Aggressive late-apex snap/oversteer |
| Record crash | **214 g** (peak) | 2003 IndyCar — Bräck at Texas |

- **Vector nature**: G-force is a vector. The total G experienced by the driver is the **vector sum** of all gravitational and non-gravitational forces — lateral, longitudinal, and vertical combined.

> **Writing tip**: Contextualize numbers. "Four lateral g" is roughly what a fighter pilot experiences in a sustained high-G turn — but in a Formula car, it's held for seconds through a compound corner.

## 3. Centripetal Acceleration: The Physics of Turning

**Centripetal force** (from Latin *centrum* "center" + *petere* "to seek") keeps a body moving along a curved path, always directed **orthogonally to the velocity** and toward the instantaneous center of curvature. First derived mathematically by **Christiaan Huygens** in 1659.

- **Equation**: $F_c = \frac{mv^2}{r}$ → therefore $a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}$
- **G-load conversion**: Centripetal acceleration in G-units = $\frac{v^2}{r \times g}$, where $g$ = 9.80665 m/s²
- The tires provide the centripetal force via **lateral friction** with the track surface: $F_{\text{max}} = \mu \times N$
- The driver feels this as a lateral push pressing them toward the outside of the corner.

### How It Applies to Cornering
1. **Entry**: Driver brakes straight, then turns in — tires transition from longitudinal (braking) to lateral (centripetal) force demand.
2. **Mid-corner**: Maximum lateral g sustained. Car is in quasi-equilibrium — centripetal demand equals available tire grip.
3. **Exit**: Throttle applied — some lateral grip converts to longitudinal (traction) force. Path radius tightens or speed increases.

> **Key concept — the friction circle**: A tire's total grip is a finite resource. Braking, acceleration, and cornering all draw from the same pool. Under braking while turning, the total demand can exceed the friction circle even if neither component alone is at maximum. Elite drivers maximize the circle by smoothly blending inputs.

## 4. The Role of Aerodynamic Downforce

**Downforce** is the aerodynamic tool that *enables* higher cornering g-levels. It is a **downward aerodynamic lift force** generated by wings, diffusers, and floorplanes.

- Downforce effectively increases the **normal force** between tires and track without adding mass.
- Maximum friction force = **μ × N**. By increasing *N* via downforce, the car sustains **higher lateral G-loads** without exceeding the tire's friction limit.
- **Speed dependency**: Downforce scales with the **square of velocity** ($F_{\text{down}} \propto v^2$). At twice the speed, four times the downforce. This is why high-speed corners (e.g., 130R at Suzuka) generate extreme lateral G-loads.
- In Formula 1, peak downforce can exceed **5× the car's static weight** at high speed.

### Downforce vs. Drag Trade-Off
| Configuration | Downforce | Drag | Best For |
|---------------|-----------|------|----------|
| High (race) | High | High | Slow, twisty circuits (Monaco, Singapore) |
| Low (speed) | Low | Low | Fast circuits with long straights (Monza, Spa) |

- **Front/rear balance**: Affects cornering balance (understeer/oversteer). Engineers tune wing angles and diffuser profiles for desired G-load distribution.
- **Energy cost**: Generating downforce creates **aerodynamic drag**, costing straight-line speed.

> **Writing angle**: Was that 5 g loaded on a high-downforce setup, or extracted from a lower-downforce balance? That distinction tells the story of strategic and setup choice.

## 5. Physical Demands on the Driver

Human tolerance depends on **magnitude**, **duration**, **direction**, **location of application**, and **posture**. G-tolerance is partially **trainable**, with considerable individual variation. Cardiovascular problems reduce tolerance.

### Horizontal G-Loads ("Eyeballs In" — Perpendicular to Spine)
The body is **best suited** for g-forces perpendicular to the spine. Untrained humans can withstand approximately:
- **20 g** for less than 10 seconds
- **10 g** for ~1 minute
- **6 g** for ~10 minutes
- With cognitive facilities intact at all levels — subjects could perform simple tasks.

**Record**: John Stapp survived a peak of **46.2 g** ("eyeballs-out") and **25+ g** for 1.1 seconds in his 1954 rocket-sled experiments — proving human limits far exceed everyday expectations.

### Vertical G-Loads (Along the Spine — Positive)
The progression as positive vertical g increases:
1. **Grey-out** — vision loses hue; reversible upon levelling out
2. **Tunnel vision** — peripheral vision progressively lost
3. **Blackout** — vision lost while consciousness is maintained (dangerous!)
4. **G-LOC** — g-induced loss of consciousness
5. **Death** — if g-forces are not quickly reduced

- Typical person: ~**5 g₀** before blackout
- Modern pilots with g-suits + straining maneuvers: **9 g₀** sustained
- Racing: curb strikes and jumps can produce sudden vertical spikes

### Negative G-Forces ("Red Out")
- Tolerance is **much lower** than positive g: typically **−2 to −3 g₀**
- Blood driven toward the head causes **red out** — blood-laden eyelids pulled into the visual field
- Blood vessels in eyes or brain may **swell or burst**, causing degraded sight or blindness
- Generally **unpleasant and potentially damaging**

### Cognitive & Motor Demands
- **Reaction time** degrades measurably above 3 g sustained
- **Neck strain**: At 4–5 g lateral, a driver's head (~5 kg) effectively weighs **20–25 kg** laterally. The neck must hold the head against this load through every corner — and a full race involves **thousands** of cornering inputs.
- **Core stability** is critical for maintaining pedal control and breathing efficiency under sustained G-loads.
- **Blood pooling** in lower extremities reduces brain perfusion even at 4–5 g.

### Record-Breaking G-Forces in Motorsport
- **Highest recorded human-survived G-force**: **214 g₀** — IndyCar driver **Kenny Bräck**, 2003 Chevy 500 at Texas Motor Speedway. Wheel-to-wheel contact sent his car into the catch fence. He survived.
- This is a testament to modern survival cell design and the body's ability to withstand momentary, properly direction-loaded forces when the driver is braced.

## 6. Measurement & Technology

- **Accelerometers** are the primary tool for measuring G-force in racing — multi-axis units capture lateral, longitudinal, and vertical loads simultaneously.
- Calibrated to measure g-force along one or more axes.
- In-vehicle **G-loggers** record data at high sample rates for post-race analysis.
- A stationary single-axis accelerometer oriented horizontally outputs **0 g** at constant velocity; **+1 g** when vertical (stationary); responsive to brake/gas inputs.
- Three-axis accelerometers in free fall output **zero-g** on all axes — the principle behind ballistictrajectory measurement.
- Engineers use this data to **correlate driver feedback** with objective measurements — essential for setup optimization.

## 7. Quick-Reference Formulae

| Quantity | Formula | Variables |
|----------|---------|-----------|
| Standard gravity | $g = 9.80665\ \text{m/s}^2$ | Constant at Earth's surface |
| Lateral (centripetal) acceleration | $a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}$ | v = speed (m/s), r = corner radius (m) |
| G-load conversion | $G = \frac{a}{g}$ | Divide m/s² by 9.80665 |
| Speed from G & radius | $v = \sqrt{G \times g \times r}$ | Corner entry speed estimate |
| Braking distance at 1 g | $s = \frac{v^2}{2g}$ | ≈ 20 m from 20 m/s; scales with v² |
| Max cornering force | $F_{\text{max}} = \mu \times N$ | μ = friction coeff., N = vertical load |
| Downforce scaling | $F_{\text{down}} \propto v^2$ | Doubles speed → 4× downforce |
