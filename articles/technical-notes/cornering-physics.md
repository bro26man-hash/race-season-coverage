# Cornering Physics — Technical Reference

> Source: Wikipedia — [G-force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-force)
> Purpose: Ready-reference facts for race season cornering analysis.

---

## 1. What Is a G-Force?

- The **g-force** (gravitational force equivalent) is a **mass-specific force** — force per unit mass — expressed in units of standard gravity (symbol **g** or **g₀**).
- One **1 g** equals the conventional gravitational acceleration on Earth's surface: **≈ 9.80665 m/s²** (≈ 35.3 km/h change in velocity per second).
- G-force is **not** a force in the Newtonian sense — it is an **acceleration** that must be produced by a **mechanical contact force**. Objects in free fall experience **0 g** (weightlessness).
- The g-force felt by any object is the **vector sum of all gravitational and non-gravitational forces** acting on it, transmitted through surface contacts.
- G-force produces **compressive stress** (pushing together) and **tensile stress** (pulling apart) within objects. The direction of the resulting weight-force is opposite to the sign of the g-force acceleration.
- **Newton's Second Law**: F = ma — the force on the driver equals their mass times the g-force acceleration. The reaction force (what the seat pushes back with) is equal and opposite (Newton's Third Law).
- **Key insight**: Regardless of whether a g-force is caused by mechanical resistance to gravity or by coordinate acceleration (change in velocity), the mechanical stresses on the body are **identical**. A driver experiencing 5 g lateral feels exactly the same physical load as someone standing on a planet with 5× Earth's gravity.

## 2. Lateral G-Loads Through Corners

- When a car changes direction (cornering), occupants experience **lateral (side-to-side) g-forces** produced by the mechanical push of the seat and harness against the body.
- Lateral g-force is the **centripetal acceleration** required to keep the car moving along a curved path: **a = v² / r** (velocity squared divided by radius of curvature).
- The faster the car or the tighter the corner, the greater the lateral g-load.
- A dragster accelerating in a straight line can exert a **horizontal g-force of 5.3 g**; cornering loads in F1 and IndyCar routinely reach **4–6 g** at maximum.
- The direction of the g-force vector determines the **sensation of weight**: positive g-force pushes the driver **into the seat** (compressive stress); negative g-force pulls the driver **out of the seat** (tensile stress).
- Unopposed acceleration due to mechanical forces is experienced whenever anyone rides in a vehicle — the seat produces lateral or longitudinal forces through mechanical push.
- **For constant acceleration over a distance s**: a = v² / (2s). This means cornering G-loads scale with the **square of speed** — doubling entry speed quadruples the G-force required to redirect the car in the same distance.
- **Load transfer**: Lateral G-loads cause weight transfer to the outside wheels, reducing grip on the inside wheels — a critical factor in cornering behavior and understeer/oversteer balance.
- **Velocity-to-G conversion**: At 1 g, velocity changes ~35 km/h (22 mph) per second. A car braking at 1 g from 105 km/h (65 mph) can stop in 3 seconds. This same rate applies to lateral cornering — the driver "feels" the speed being redirected, not just the speed being shed.

## 3. Centripetal Acceleration

- Centripetal acceleration is always directed **toward the center of curvature** of the path.
- It is what the driver and car *feel* as the lateral push during cornering — the car's tires generate the centripetal force via grip against the track surface.
- The magnitude scales with the **square of speed**: doubling speed quadruples the centripetal acceleration required for the same radius.
- This is why **speed squared over radius (v²/r)** is the fundamental cornering equation — small increases in entry speed demand disproportionately higher mechanical grip.
- In a car, this force is transmitted through the **seat, harness, and tires**; the driver's body must withstand the resulting load.
- **Combined G-loads**: In a corner, the driver feels a combined G-vector — lateral cornering G (pushing sideways) superimposed on any longitudinal G (braking or accelerating). The vector sum determines the total G-force the driver experiences.
- **Jerk** (rate of change of acceleration, m/s³ or g₀/s) is critical in cornering because sudden increases in lateral G — such as hitting a bump or cresting a kerb — can cause loss of control or disorientation before the driver can react. A stiff object dropped from 1 m and stopping in 1 mm experiences 1000 g; the same principle applies to sudden impacts on a car's suspension during cornering.
- **Driver technique**: A driver who lifts off the throttle mid-corner reduces longitudinal acceleration, shifting the total G-vector purely lateral. This can momentarily increase the lateral G-limit before tires reach their peak.

## 4. The Role of Downforce

- **Downforce** is the aerodynamic force pushing the car **into the track**, increasing the normal force (N) available for tire grip.
- Since **friction = μ × N**, more downforce means more available grip, allowing higher cornering speeds before the tires reach their limit.
- Downforce scales with **speed squared** — a car generates dramatically more downforce at 300 km/h than at 100 km/h.
- **Slow corners**: Less downforce → less grip → drivers must accept lower entry speeds or carry more speed through the apex.
- **High-speed corners**: Massive downforce → enormous lateral G-loads → the driver must withstand forces that push the physical limits of human tolerance.
- Wings, diffusers, and underbody ground effect are all designed to manage this aero load.
- Downforce also shifts the **balance of G-loads** between axles. Under braking, front-axis downforce increases (load transfer), giving front-biased grip. Under acceleration, rear-axis loading increases.
- **Adjustable aerodynamics** (front splitters, rear wings, DRS) allow teams to tune the downforce-G tradeoff for each circuit: high-downforce setups for twisty tracks (more cornering G), low-downrift setups for speed circuits (less drag, less cornering G but higher top speed).
- **The fundamental tradeoff**: More downforce = more cornering G capacity, but also more aerodynamic drag = longer straights and more braking demand. Teams must balance this for each circuit's character.
- Without downforce, tire friction alone limits cornering to roughly 1–1.5 g; with modern aero, cars can sustain **5–6 g** through high-speed corners.

## 5. Physical Demands on Drivers

### Vertical G-Tolerance (Spine-Aligned)
- Blood pressure varies significantly along the body under vertical g, limiting maximum tolerance.
- A typical person can handle about **5 g** before losing consciousness.
- With **g-suits and muscle straining** (forcing blood back to the brain), modern pilots can sustain **≈ 9 g**.
- Progressive symptoms under increasing positive vertical g:
  1. **Grey-out** — vision loses hue (easily reversible on levelling out)
  2. **Tunnel vision** — peripheral vision progressively lost
  3. **Blackout** — loss of vision while consciousness is maintained (blood flow to head insufficient)
  4. **G-LOC** — g-force-induced loss of consciousness
  5. **Death** — if g-forces are not quickly reduced
- Resistance to **negative** (downward) g is much lower: typically **−2 to −3 g** limit. This causes "red out" where vision reddens due to blood-laden eyelids being pulled into the field of vision. Negative g is generally unpleasant and can cause damage — blood vessels in the eyes or brain may swell or burst.
- Some g-tolerance is **trainable**; there is also considerable **variation in innate ability** between individuals. **Cardiovascular problems** reduce g-tolerance.

### Horizontal G-Tolerance (Perpendicular to Spine)
- The body survives **eyeballs-in** (forward acceleration, lying on back) far better than **eyeballs-out** (rearward acceleration, lying on front) since blood vessels in the retina are more sensitive in the latter direction.
- Untrained humans tolerate roughly:
  - **20 g** for less than 10 seconds
  - **10 g** for 1 minute
  - **6 g** for 10 minutes
- These forces were endured with cognitive facilities intact — subjects could perform simple physical and communication tasks.
- **Record holder**: John Stapp survived **46.2 g** peak in a late 1954 rocket-sled deceleration test (speed went from Mach 0.9 to zero in just over a second). He lived another 45 years to age 89 with no ill effects.
- **Highest recorded g-force in racing**: **214 g** — Kenny Bräck, 2003 Chevy 500 at Texas Motor Speedway, after catch-fence impact following wheel-to-wheel contact with Tomas Scheckter's car.

### Practical Racing Implications
- **Cornering** primarily loads the driver **laterally** (eyeballs-out direction), which is less tolerable than eyeballs-in.
- **Braking** loads longitudinally forward (eyeballs-in), better tolerated.
- **Acceleration** (out of corners) loads longitudinally rearward (eyeballs-out), moderately tolerable.
- **Neck strain** is critical: at 5 g lateral, the neck must support an effective head weight of ~25 kg — stronger drivers survive longer careers.
- **Vision impact**: lateral g shifts blood in the retina, causing progressive vision degradation — drivers must maintain awareness through grey-out and tunnel vision.
- **G-hardening**: Preparing the body (and equipment) to withstand high g-forces without damage is called g-hardening — this applies to drivers, instruments, and onboard equipment.
- **Shock vs. sustained g**: Impact and mechanical shock involve high-kinetic-energy, short-term excitation. A collision can produce transient g-forces far exceeding sustained cornering loads (as the 214 g record shows).

## 6. Key Equations Quick Reference

| Quantity | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Standard gravity | g₀ = 9.80665 m/s² | Earth surface baseline; does not vary by location |
| Centripetal acceleration | a = v² / r | v = speed, r = corner radius |
| Constant accel. over distance | a = v² / (2s) | Useful for braking zone estimates |
| Kinetic energy | E = ½mv² | Speed doubles → energy quadruples |
| Braking distance at 1 g | d = v² / (2g) | e.g., 35 km/h → stops in 1 sec at 1 g |
| Downforce scaling | F_down ∝ v² | Double speed = 4× downforce |
| Weight under g-force | W = m × (−g-force) | Negative sign: force opposes acceleration direction |
| Impulse / shock | F × Δt = m × Δv | Short duration = peak force amplification |

## 7. Measurement

- G-force is measured using an **accelerometer** — a damped mass on a spring, measuring displacement along one or more axes.
- A stationary, single-axis accelerometer oriented horizontally outputs **0 g** on a level road at constant velocity.
- Rotated 90° vertical, it reads **+1 g** upwards even when stationary — measuring the mechanical support force, not gravity directly.
- In free fall (ballistic trajectory), a three-axis accelerometer outputs **zero-g** on all axes — confirming that g-force measures surface-contact forces, not gravitation.
- Race teams use **multi-axis accelerometers** (longitudinal, lateral, vertical) mounted in the car and on the driver's helmet to quantify real-world g-loads for every corner, brake zone, and acceleration point.

## Writing Tips for Cornering Analysis

1. **Anchor with numbers**: "Turn 3 carries 5.2 g at 285 km/h — that's over 4× the downforce the car generates at 150 km/h."
2. **Humanize the physics**: "At that load, the driver's head effectively weighs 25 kg, and their peripheral vision is narrowing into tunnel vision."
3. **Compare corners**: Use v²/r to explain why a tight hairpin (small r) demands more grip than a fast sweeper (large r) even at lower speed.
4. **Contextualize downforce**: "The high-downforce setup here generates enough load to corner at 6 g — approaching the human tolerance limit for eyeballs-out lateral loading."
5. **Reference the data**: Link sector times and telemetry from `data/telemetry/` to show real-world g-load traces through the corner sequence.
6. **Distinguish shock from sustained g**: A 214 g collision is a fraction of a second — fundamentally different from 5 g sustained through a 15-second corner. Use the jerk concept to explain why sudden inputs matter.
