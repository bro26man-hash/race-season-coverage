## 1. G-Force Fundamentals

- **Definition:** G-force (gravitational force equivalent) is a **mass-specific force** — force per unit mass — expressed in units of standard gravity (symbol **g** or **g₀**). It is *not* actually a force in the strict physics sense; it is a measure of **acceleration relative to free fall**.
- **Standard gravity:** 1 g = **9.80665 m/s²** (≈ 35.3 km/h or 22 mph of velocity change per second).
- **Key distinction:** Gravitational acceleration alone does *not* produce g-force. Objects in free fall experience **0 g** (weightlessness). G-force only arises from **mechanical pushes and pulls** — surface-contact forces transmitted through objects via interior mechanical stresses.
- **Sign convention:** Positive g-force points downward (upward acceleration), producing a compressive force pushing the driver into the seat. Negative g-force points upward, producing a force that pulls the driver upward — potentially lifting them out of the seat.

## 2. Lateral G-Loads Through Corners

- When a vehicle changes **direction** (not just speed), occupants experience **lateral (side-to-side) g-forces** generated by the mechanical push of the seat and tire contact with the track surface.
- Lateral g-force is the **centripetal acceleration** directed toward the center of the corner's arc. It is governed by:

  > **a = v² / r**

  Where *v* = corner-entry speed and *r* = radius of curvature. This means **doubling entry speed quadruples the lateral g-load**, and tighter radius (sharper corner) dramatically increases loading.
- A typical Formula 1 car experiences **4–6 g of lateral load** through high-speed corners (e.g., Copse at Silverstone, Turn 8 at Istanbul).
- Lateral g is perpendicular to the spine for a driver seated upright, meaning the body is loaded from side to side rather than head-to-foot — this is generally **better tolerated** than vertical g, but still imposes significant muscular and skeletal demands.

## 3. Centripetal Acceleration & Cornering Dynamics

- **Centripetal (radial/normal) acceleration** is the component of acceleration that changes the *direction* of an object's velocity without changing its speed — it always points toward the center of the circular path.
- In cornering, the tires must generate a **centripetal force** equal to *m × v² / r* to keep the car on its arc. This force is supplied entirely by **tire grip** (lateral friction coefficient).
- **The grip limit:** If the required centripetal force exceeds what the tires can provide (μ × N, where μ is the lateral friction coefficient and N is the vertical load), the car **slides out** of the corner.
- **Downforce directly increases N**, which proportionally increases the maximum available centripetal force — allowing higher corner speeds for the same tire.
- Together with longitudinal (braking/accelerating) loads, lateral g is combined into a **resultant load vector** that the tires must handle — under braking while turning, the total demand can exceed the friction circle limit even if neither individual component is at its maximum.

## 4. The Role of Downforce

- **Downforce** is a **downwards aerodynamic lift force** generated by wings, diffusers, and other aerodynamic surfaces. Its purpose is to **increase the vertical load on the tires**, thereby creating **more mechanical grip**.
- Unlike aerodynamic lift in aviation (which seeks to raise the wing), downforce deliberately pushes the car **into the track** — effectively increasing the "weight" of the car without adding mass.
- **Key relationship:** Maximum lateral cornering force = μ × (car weight + downforce). At high speed, downforce can be **several times the car's static weight** in top categories, meaning the effective grip available is dramatically amplified.
- **Trade-off:** Aerodynamic devices that generate downforce also produce **aerodynamic drag**, which limits top speed on straights. Teams constantly balance **downforce level** against **drag** for circuit-specific setup.
- **Speed dependency:** Downforce scales approximately with the **square of velocity** — at twice the speed, four times the downforce. This means cornering capability grows dramatically with speed, but so does the drag penalty.

## 5. Physical Demands on Drivers

### G-Tolerance by Direction

| Direction | Description | Typical Human Limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Vertical (+g, up)** | Blood driven downward to feet; vision/brain effects first | ~5 g₀ untrained; **9 g₀ sustained** with g-suits + muscle straining |
| **Vertical (−g, down)** | Blood driven to head; "red out" — blood reddening eyelids | **−2 to −3 g₀** (much lower tolerance; generally unpleasant/damaging) |
| **Horizontal (eyeballs in, chest-to-seat)** | Lateral cornering load; better tolerated perpendicular to spine | **20 g₀ for <10 s; 10 g₀ for 1 min; 6 g₀ for 10 min** |
| **Horizontal (eyeballs out, back-to-seat)** | Rearward acceleration; retinal blood vessels more vulnerable | Lower than eyeballs-in; sustained tolerance significantly reduced |

### Physiological Effects During Cornering

- **Grey-out:** Vision loses hue (reversible on straightening up) — early warning of inadequate blood flow to the retina under +g.
- **Tunnel vision:** Peripheral vision progressively lost — a more severe stage preceding blackout.
- **Blackout:** Complete vision loss while consciousness is maintained — caused by acute lack of blood flow to the head.
- **G-LOC (G-induced Loss of Consciousness):** Full unconsciousness from sustained high g; **life-threatening** if not quickly reduced (e.g., in a crash).
- **Neck & core strain:** Lateral cornering loads still require significant **neck muscle activation** to keep the head stable and **core engagement** to transfer loads between helmet, seat, and pedals. Over a full race stint, this accumulates as **localized fatigue**.
- **Cardiovascular stress:** Sustained lateral g loads require the circulatory system to work harder to maintain brain perfusion, especially combined with elevated heart rate from physical exertion (heart rates of **120–170 bpm** are common during race stints).
- **Vision impacts:** Even at lateral g levels well below loss-of-consciousness thresholds, **visual accuracy degrades** — affecting peripheral detection of other cars, curbing, and track position.

### Record-Setting Data Points

- **Highest recorded g-force ever survived by a human:** **214 g₀** — Kenny Bräck, 2003 IndyCar Series finale at Texas Motor Speedway (wheel-to-wheel collision into catch fence). Survived, but extreme case of very short-duration shock.
- **John Stapp's rocket-sled experiments (1954):** Withstood **46.2 g₀ eyeballs-out** for 1.1 seconds — peak experimental horizontal tolerance record.
- **Dragster horizontal acceleration:** Can produce **5.3 g** during launch — purely longitudinal.

## 6. Practical Implications for Cornering Analysis

1. **Corner-entry speed is king:** Because lateral g scales with v², a 10% increase in entry speed produces a **21% increase in cornering g-load**. This is why exit speed concepts (sacrifice entry for early power application) are critical.
2. **Line choice changes effective radius:** A tighter racing line (smaller r) demands higher g for the same speed — but may enable earlier power application, reducing total time.
3. **Downforce setup is circuit-specific:** High-downforce circuits (Monaco, Singapore) produce massive cornering grip at low speeds; low-downforce circuits (Monza, Spa) require much higher speeds to generate sufficient load.
4. **Tire degradation compounds g-loss:** As tires wear, the lateral friction coefficient (μ) drops, meaning the **same corner speed produces less g-load capacity** — drivers must either slow down or accept understeer/oversteer.
5. **Cumulative driver fatigue:** A lap with multiple high-g corners (e.g., Istanbul Turn 8, or a long Pouhon section at Spa) creates **progressive neck and core fatigue** that degrades steering precision and brake modulation in later laps.
