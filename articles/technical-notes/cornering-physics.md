# Cornering Physics: G-Force & the Demands of Speed

_Sourced from the Wikipedia article on G-force — curated for race-season coverage._

---

## 1. What Is G-Force?

- G-force (gravitational force equivalent) is a **mass-specific force** — force per unit mass — expressed in units of standard gravity (**g** or **g₀**), where **1 g = 9.80665 m/s²** (≈ 35.3 km/h per second).
- It describes the **perception of weight** under acceleration. An object at rest on Earth's surface feels 1 g upward from the ground pushing against gravity.
- **G-force is a vector** — it has magnitude and direction. Positive g-forces point downward (upward acceleration), negative g-forces point upward (downward acceleration).
- G-force **must be produced by a mechanical force** — gravity alone produces no g-force. Weightlessness (0 g) occurs in free fall.
- **Newton's Second Law (F = ma)** is the foundation: any acceleration of a mass requires a proportional force, and that force is experienced as g-load.
- **Weight = mass × −g-force** — the actual force felt is in the opposite direction to the g-force vector.

---

## 2. Lateral G-Loads Through Corners

- Whenever a vehicle **changes direction**, occupants feel **lateral (side-to-side) g-forces** produced by the mechanical push of the seat.
- In a corner, the car's inertia resists the change in direction, while the tires generate a **centripetal force** toward the center of the turn. The g-force experienced is the ratio of that centripetal force to the car's weight.
- **Formula**: lateral g = v² / (r × g₀), where *v* is speed and *r* is turn radius. Higher speed or tighter radius = higher lateral g.
- A dragster can exert a **horizontal g-force of 5.3 g** under full acceleration — illustrating the magnitude possible in high-performance scenarios.
- The human body is **better at surviving g-forces perpendicular to the spine** (lateral/eyeballs-in) than along the spine (vertical). This is why "eyeballs-in" lateral g is more tolerable than vertical g.
- Untrained humans can tolerate approximately **20 g for < 10 seconds**, **10 g for 1 minute**, or **6 g for 10 minutes** laterally — with cognitive function intact.

---

## 3. Centripetal Acceleration & Cornering

- **Centripetal acceleration** is the acceleration directed toward the center of a curved path. In racing, this is the core physics of cornering: a = v² / r.
- The g-force felt in a corner is essentially the **centripetal acceleration expressed in g-units** — a car pulling 2 g laterally is experiencing a centripetal acceleration of 2 × 9.80665 m/s².
- At the **highest recorded crash g-force**, Kenny Bräck's car at the 2003 IndyCar Series finale at Texas Motor Speedway hit the catch fence at **214 g** — a tragic reminder of the extreme forces in motorsport.
- **Jerk** (rate of change of acceleration, in m/s³ or g/s) is critical in racing — sudden changes in cornering load (on entry or exit) create spikes in jerk that can unsettle the car and stress the driver's body.
- A stiff object dropped from 1 m impacting over 1 mm experiences **1,000 g deceleration** — illustrating how short-duration shocks amplify g-levels enormously.

---

## 4. The Role of Downforce

- While the G-force article does not cover downforce explicitly, the principle is directly related: **downforce is an aerodynamic mechanical force pushing the car into the track**, increasing the normal load on the tires.
- More downforce → more tire grip → **higher centripetal acceleration possible** without slipping → higher lateral g through corners.
- Downforce effectively **increases the "g-weight" of the car** onto the surface, letting the tires exert greater cornering force.
- The relationship: **g-force in corners is proportional to downforce**; F1 cars can generate lateral accelerations of **4–6 g** in fast corners, largely enabled by massive aerodynamic downforce.
- In the G-force framework, downforce adds to the contact force between tire and road — the same surface-contact force that produces the g-load felt by the driver.

## 5. Physical Demands on Drivers

### G-LOC & Vision Degradation (Vertical G)
- **Positive (upward) g-force** drives blood downward, starving the brain and eyes.
- Progressive symptoms: **Grey-out → Tunnel vision → Blackout → G-LOC (g-force induced loss of consciousness)**.
- A typical person loses consciousness at about **5 g** sustained; with g-suits and muscle straining, trained pilots/racers handle **9 g sustained**.
- **Negative (downward) g** drives blood to the head, causing **red-out** and potential vessel damage — tolerance is lower (−2 to −3 g).

### Lateral G & Cognitive Performance
- Horizontal g-forces are better tolerated: **20 g for < 10 s**, **10 g for 1 min**, **6 g for 10 min** with intact cognition.
- This is why cornering g-loads are survivable — but **sustained high lateral g** over a full stint still creates cumulative fatigue.

### Human Factors in Racing
- **G-tolerance is trainable** but varies individually; cardiovascular fitness is a key factor.
- **Vibration at resonant frequencies** of organs or connective tissues can be severely damaging even at low peak g.
- Neck and core muscles must resist enormous head and upper-body loads under lateral g — a key fitness requirement for F1 and IndyCar drivers.
- The **record peak horizontal g-force survived** by a human: **46.2 g** (John Stapp, 1954 rocket sled), and the **highest crash-survived g-force**: **214 g** (Kenny Bräck, 2003 IndyCar).

### Mechanical Stress on the Body
- Compressive and tensile stresses spread from contact points (seat, belts, pedals) — the driver's spine is the critical load path.
- Arms experience tensile stress from being braced against lateral load; legs experience compressive stress from pedal loads.
- The faster the g-change (jerk), the more dangerous — high-jerk events like curb strikes or sudden direction changes are particularly punishing.
