# Cornering Physics: Technical Reference Notes

> Source: Wikipedia — [G-force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-force)
> Purpose: Ready-reference facts for race-season cornering analysis.

---

## 1. G-Force Fundamentals

- **Definition:** G-force (gravitational force equivalent) is a **mass-specific force** — force per unit mass — expressed in units of standard gravity (symbol *g* or *g₀*). One *g* equals 9.80665 m/s², the acceleration due to gravity at Earth's surface.
- **What produces g-force:** G-force is produced only by **mechanical contact forces** (pushes and pulls from surfaces). Gravitation alone does *not* produce g-force — an object in free fall experiences 0 g regardless of how fast it's going. This is why a driver feels pinned into the seat through a corner, not because of gravity, but because the seat is pushing them sideways.
- **Direction conventions:** Positive g-force points downward (producing a sensation of weight pressing into the seat). Negative g-force points upward (producing a sensation of being lifted out of the seat). In cornering, the critical loads are **lateral** — side-to-side — perpendicular to the direction of travel.

## 2. Lateral G-Loads Through Corners

- **Physics of the corner:** When a car turns, it undergoes **centripetal acceleration** directed toward the center of the curve. The magnitude is *a = v²/r* (velocity squared divided by the radius of curvature). This acceleration is what generates the lateral g-load felt by the driver and transmitted through the seat and restraints.
- **Perception of weight:** The g-force in a corner creates an operational sensation of weight — the driver feels effectively "heavier" in the sideways direction. A 2 g lateral corner means the driver's body experiences a sideways force equal to **twice its normal weight**.
- **Dragster reference:** A top fuel dragster can exert a **horizontal g-force of ~5.3 g** during acceleration. While this is longitudinal (braking/acceleration), it demonstrates the magnitude of mechanical forces the human body can be subjected to in motorsport.
- **Combined loading:** In real cornering scenarios, drivers often experience **combined g-loads** — lateral g through the turn plus longitudinal g from braking or accelerating out. The total g-force is the vector sum of all accelerations acting on the body.

## 3. Centripetal Acceleration & Cornering Speed

- **Key relationship:** Centripetal acceleration = *v² / r*. This means that **doubling speed quadruples the lateral g-load** for the same corner radius. A corner that produces 2 g at 100 km/h will produce 8 g at 200 km/h — a critical reason why track limits and speed management are safety issues.
- **Radius matters:** Tighter corners (smaller *r*) generate higher g-loads at the same speed. This is why hairpins are the most physically demanding corners — drivers can experience **3–5+ g** in slow-speed hairpins depending on the car.
- **The physics equation in practice:** *F = ma* — the centripetal force required is proportional to both the car's mass and the centripetal acceleration. Downforce and tire grip together must provide this force, or the car will not make the corner.

## 4. The Role of Downforce

- **What it does:** Downforce is the **aerodynamic downward force** generated by the car's wings, floor, and body shape. It acts like inverted lift — pushing the car into the track rather than letting it fly off.
- **How it enables cornering:** Downforce increases the **normal force** between tires and track surface. Since tire grip (friction) is proportional to normal force (*F_friction = μ × F_normal*), more downforce means more available grip to sustain higher centripetal acceleration — i.e., higher cornering speeds at the same g-level.
- **Speed-dependent force:** Unlike mechanical downforce (springs, anti-roll bars), aerodynamic downforce scales with **the square of speed**. At twice the speed, downforce is four times greater. This is why high-speed corners (e.g., 130R at Suzuka, Pouhon at Spa) generate enormous lateral g-loads — the aero is working at peak efficiency.
- **Trade-off — drag vs. downforce:** Wing and aero setups are a compromise. More downforce for cornering grip means more **aerodynamic drag**, which costs straight-line speed. Teams tune this balance for each circuit — high-downforce for twisty Monaco, low-downforce for fast Monza.
- **IndyCar example — the 214 g impact:** The highest recorded g-force survived by a human occurred during the **2003 IndyCar race at Texas Motor Speedway**, when Kenny Bräck's car hit the catch fence at **214 g peak** after wheel-to-wheel contact. This extreme transverse load far exceeded sustained cornering g-orders and illustrates the structural limits of force transmission.

## 5. Physical Demands on Drivers

### G-Tolerance by Direction

- **Lateral (perpendicular to spine) — "eyeballs in" vs. "eyeballs out":**
  - The human body tolerates lateral g-forces **much better** than vertical g-forces because blood circulation is less disrupted when the acceleration is perpendicular to the spine.
  - **"Eyeballs in"** (acceleration forwards, driver lying back): Untrained humans can tolerate up to **20 g for <10 seconds**, **10 g for 1 minute**, and **6 g for 10 minutes** with cognitive faculties intact.
  - **"Eyeballs out"** (acceleration backwards, driver pushed forward): Retinal blood vessels are more sensitive; tolerance is somewhat lower, though still substantial for short durations.
  - **Record holder:** John Stapp survived **46.2 g peak** ("eyeballs-out") and over **25 g for 1.1 seconds** in his 1954 rocket-sled experiments — proof of remarkable human tolerance to brief lateral loads.

- **Vertical (along the spine) — for comparison:**
  - Typical person: ~**5 g** before losing consciousness.
  - Modern pilots with g-suits and straining maneuvers: sustained **9 g**.
  - Progressive symptoms: **Grey-out** (vision loses hue) → **Tunnel vision** (peripheral vision lost) → **Blackout** (vision lost, consciousness maintained) → **G-LOC** (g-force-induced loss of consciousness).
  - **Negative g** (downward, blood to head): Tolerance is only **−2 to −3 g**; can cause **red out** (vision reddened) and potential retinal/brain vessel damage.

### What Cornering G-Loads Actually Do to a Driver

1. **Blood redistribution:** Lateral g-forces shift blood toward the outside of the turn. At sustained 4–5 g, this can impair cognitive function and slow reaction times — critical in wheel-to-wheel racing.
2. **Neck strain:** The driver's head weighs roughly 4–5 kg. At 4 g lateral, the neck must support an equivalent lateral force of **~16–20 kg** repeatedly through a session. This is why neck conditioning is essential.
3. **Vision effects:** Even lateral g-loads can cause mild **grey-out** under extreme conditions because the vestibular system (inner ear) is affected, though less dramatically than vertical g.
4. **Core & upper-body fatigue:** Sustaining deliberate muscle straining (the "g-strain maneuver") through a 10-lap stint at 3–4 g lateral is an immense workout. Drivers lose **1–2 kg of body mass** per race through sweat and exertion.
5. **Reaction time degradation:** Research shows that at sustained 3+ g, cognitive processing speed drops measurably. Drivers must compensate through **anticipation and muscle memory** developed over thousands of laps.

### G-Hardening & Training

- **G-hardening** is the process of preparing the body to withstand high g-forces without degradation. In motorsport, this includes:
  - **Neck resistance training** (weighted head rotations, neck harnesses)
  - **Cardiovascular conditioning** to maintain blood pressure under load
  - **Core strengthening** to stabilize the torso against lateral forces
  - **High-G centrifuge sessions** (used by some F1 and IndyCar teams)
- **Individual variation:** G-tolerance varies significantly between individuals and can be improved with training. Cardiovascular health is a major factor — poor conditioning reduces tolerance substantially.

## 6. Practical Applications for Race Coverage

- **Qualifying vs. race pace:** Qualifying laps often produce **higher peak g-loads** (maximum attack, lower fuel, softer tires). Race-pace g-loads are lower but sustained over many laps — the cumulative physical toll is the story.
- **Circuit character:** Compare the **sustained medium-speed corners** of Bahrain (5–6 g in Turns 1 & 3) vs. the **explosive high-g bursts** of Singapore (6–7 g in hairpins). Each circuit tells a different physical story.
- **Car evolution:** As regulations change (e.g., ground-effect floors returning in 2022), the **balance of downforce** shifts — more mechanical grip lessens tire-dependent g-loads, while aero-dependent grip makes cornering forces more speed-sensitive.
- **Telemetry storytelling:** Use accelerometer data (see `../data/telemetry/`) to **graph g-load traces** for each sector. A lap chart of lateral g-over-time reveals where a driver is hurting the most and where they're managing effort.
