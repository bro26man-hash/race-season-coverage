# Cornering Physics: G-Force Reference Notes

> Source: Wikipedia — [G-force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-force)
> Purpose: Technical reference for race-season cornering analysis articles.

---

## 1. G-Force Fundamentals

- **Definition**: G-force (gravitational force equivalent) is a **mass-specific force** — force per unit mass — expressed in units of standard gravity (symbol **g** or **g₀**). It is *not* actually a force but an **acceleration** measured relative to free fall.
- **Standard gravity**: 1 g = **9.80665 m/s²** (≈ 35.3 km/h change in velocity per second). This value does not vary by location; on the Moon it's ≈ 1/6 of Earth's.
- **Key distinction**: Gravitation alone does **not** produce g-force. Only **mechanical pushes and pulls** (surface-contact forces) generate g-force. A driver in free fall feels weightless (0 g), even though gravity is still acting.
- **Positive vs. negative g**: Positive g-force points downward (upward acceleration), producing compressive stress. Negative g-force points upward (downward acceleration), producing tensile stress — the kind that pulls a driver out of the seat.
- **Newton's Second Law**: **F = ma** — the force on the driver equals their mass times the g-force acceleration. The actual reaction force (what the seat pushes back with) is equal and opposite (Newton's Third Law).
- **Why it matters**: Regardless of whether a g-force is caused by mechanical resistance to gravity or by coordinate acceleration (change in velocity), the mechanical stresses on the body are **identical**. A driver experiencing 5 g lateral in a corner feels exactly the same physical load as someone standing on a planet with 5× Earth's gravity.

## 2. Lateral G-Loads Through Corners

- When a vehicle **changes direction**, occupants experience **lateral (side-to-side) g-forces** generated by the mechanical push of the seat against the body.
- Lateral g-forces are **perpendicular to the spine** when the driver is seated upright — this is the "eyeballs in" orientation for a left-hand corner (body pushed to the right) or "eyeballs out" for a right-hand corner (body pushed to the left).
- **The body tolerates lateral g-forces far better along the spine axis than perpendicular to it.** "Eyeballs in" acceleration (subject lying on their back) shows significantly higher tolerance than "eyeballs out" (lying on their front), because blood vessels in the retina are more sensitive in the latter direction.
- **Formula context**: For constant acceleration over a braking or cornering distance *s*, the acceleration is **v² / (2s)**. This means cornering G-loads scale with the **square of speed** — doubling entry speed quadruples the G-force required to stop or redirect the car in the same distance.
- **Load transfer**: Lateral G-loads cause weight transfer to the outside wheels, reducing grip on the inside wheels — a critical factor in cornering behavior and understeer/oversteer balance.
- **Real-world magnitudes**:
  - Formula 1 high-speed corners: **4–6 g** lateral
  - IndyCar corners: up to **5+ g**
  - Dragsters: **5.3 g** horizontal during acceleration
- **Velocity-to-G conversion**: At 1 g, velocity changes ~35 km/h (22 mph) per second. A car braking at 1 g from 105 km/h (65 mph) can stop in 3 seconds. This same rate of change applies to lateral cornering — the driver "feels" the speed being redirected, not just the speed being shed.

## 3. Centripetal Acceleration in Cornering

- **Centripetal acceleration** is the inward-directed acceleration that keeps the car moving along a curved path. It is provided by the **lateral friction force** between tires and track surface.
- The magnitude is **a = v² / r**, where *v* is speed and *r* is the corner radius. This is directly proportional to the **square of speed** and inversely proportional to the corner radius — tighter corners demand dramatically more G-force at the same speed.
- In a corner, the driver feels a **combined G-load**: lateral cornering G (pushing sideways) superimposed on any longitudinal G (braking or accelerating). The **vector sum** of these components determines the total G-force the driver experiences.
- **Jerk** (rate of change of acceleration, measured in m/s³ or g₀/s) is critical in cornering because **sudden increases in lateral G** — such as hitting a bump or cresting a kerb — can cause loss of control or disorientation before the driver can react. A stiff object dropped from 1 m and stopping in 1 mm experiences 1000 g — the same principle applies to sudden impacts on a car's suspension during cornering.
- **Practical implication**: A driver who lifts off the throttle mid-corner reduces longitudinal acceleration, which shifts the total G-vector purely lateral. This can momentarily increase the lateral G-limit before tires reach their peak.

## 4. The Role of Downforce

- **Downforce** is the aerodynamic force pushing the car into the track, effectively **increasing the normal force (N)** on the tires.
- Since **friction = μ × N**, more downforce means more available grip, allowing higher cornering speeds before the tires reach their limit.
- However, downforce is **speed-dependent** — it scales with the square of airflow velocity. This means:
  - **Slow corners**: Less downforce → less grip → drivers must accept lower entry speeds or carry more speed through the apex.
  - **High-speed corners**: Massive downforce → enormous lateral G-loads → the driver must withstand forces that push the physical limits of human tolerance.
- Downforce also shifts the **balance of G-loads** between axles. Under braking, front-axis downforce increases (load transfer), giving front-biased grip. Under acceleration, rear-axis loading increases.
- **Adjustable aerodynamics** (front splitters, rear wings, DRS) allow teams to tune the downforce-G tradeoff for each circuit: high-downforce setups for twisty tracks (more cornering G), low-downforce setups for speed circuits (less drag, less cornering G but higher top speed).
- **The fundamental tradeoff**: More downforce = more cornering G capacity, but also more aerodynamic drag = longer straights and more braking demand. Teams must balance this for each circuit's character.

## 5. Physical Demands on the Driver

### 5.1 G-Tolerance Thresholds

| Orientation | Duration | Tolerance | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Eyeballs in** (lateral, forward) | < 10 seconds | up to **20 g₀** | Untrained humans; cognitive function intact |
| **Eyeballs in** (lateral, forward) | 1 minute | **10 g₀** | Untrained humans; can perform tasks |
| **Eyeballs in** (lateral, forward) | 10 minutes | **6 g₀** | Untrained humans; sustained tolerance |
| **Vertical** (along spine) | Sustained | **~5 g₀** | Untrained; grey-out risk begins |
| **Vertical** (along spine) | Sustained | **~9 g₀** | With g-suit + straining (modern pilots) |
| **Negative g** (downward) | Any | **−2 to −3 g₀** | "Red out"; much lower tolerance; can cause damage |

### 5.2 Progressive Symptoms of Positive G-Exposure

1. **Grey-out**: Vision loses hue; easily reversible on levelling out
2. **Tunnel vision**: Peripheral vision progressively lost; central vision remains
3. **Blackout**: Loss of vision while consciousness is maintained — caused by lack of blood flow to the head
4. **G-LOC** (G-induced Loss of Consciousness): The most dangerous outcome; driver is temporarily unconscious
5. **Death**: If g-forces are not quickly reduced

### 5.3 Negative G-Exposure (Red Out)

- Resistance to negative g is **much lower** than positive g — typically limited to −2 to −3 g₀.
- Blood rushes to the head, causing vision to literally redden (blood-laden lower eyelid pulled into field of vision).
- Blood vessels in the eyes or brain may swell or burst, resulting in degraded sight or even blindness.
- Generally unpleasant and potentially damaging even at brief exposure.

### 5.4 Physiological Mechanisms

- **Blood pressure gradient**: G-forces along the spine cause significant variation in blood pressure along the body's length, which limits maximum tolerable forces.
- **Compressive stress**: Most of the body experiences compressive stress (pushed toward the seat). The mechanical resistive force spreads from contact points and gradually decreases toward unsupported ends.
- **Tensile stress**: Unsupported body parts (head, arms hanging) experience tensile stress (pulled away from the seat). This stress gradient can cause strain injuries.
- **Vibration resonance**: Relatively low peak g-force levels can be severely damaging if they match the **resonant frequency** of organs or connective tissues.
- **Flexible body**: The human body is flexible and deformable, particularly softer tissues — meaning g-forces are not uniformly distributed.

### 5.5 Training & Mitigation

- **G-suits**: Inflatable garments that compress the legs and abdomen, forcing blood back toward the brain during positive G.
- **Muscle straining**: Drivers tense core and leg muscles to mechanically impede blood pooling.
- **Anti-G straining maneuver (AGSM)**: A specific breathing and muscle-contraction technique used by fighter pilots and increasingly by racing drivers.
- **Neck conditioning**: The head weighs ~5 kg; at 4 g lateral, effective head weight becomes ~20 kg, placing enormous torque on the cervical spine. Neck strengthening is critical.
- **Trainability**: G-tolerance can be improved with training, but there is considerable variation in innate ability between individuals. Cardiovascular health significantly affects tolerance.
- **Hydration & fatigue**: Dehydration and fatigue reduce g-tolerance. Drivers must maintain cardiovascular fitness and fluid intake.

### 5.6 Real-World Racing Benchmarks

- **Highest recorded g-force survived by a human**: **214 g₀** — Kenny Bräck, 2003 IndyCar Series finale at Texas Motor Speedway, after wheel-to-wheel contact and impact with the catch fence. This is a crash scenario, not sustained cornering.
- **Dragster horizontal acceleration**: **5.3 g** — a benchmark for peak performance acceleration.
- **F1 cornering**: Sustained **4–6 g** lateral through high-speed corners like Copse, Maggots-Becketts, or Eau Rouge.

## 7. Key Takeaways for Writers

1. **Cornering G is v²/r** — speed is the dominant variable; a 10% speed increase yields a 21% G-force increase.
2. **Downforce is the great equalizer** — it allows slower cars to corner competitively but creates brutal G-loads at high speed.
3. **The human body is direction-dependent** — lateral "eyeballs in" is tolerated far better than lateral "eyeballs out" or vertical positive G.
4. **Jerk kills** — the *rate* of G onset matters as much as the magnitude; sudden spikes cause disorientation before the driver adapts.
5. **G-LOC is the silent threat** — a driver can lose consciousness in a fraction of a second at sustained threshold loads, with potentially catastrophic consequences at 300+ km/h.
6. **The 214 g record** reminds us that crash forces dwarf cornering forces — and yet Bräck survived, a testament to both the strips and modern safety engineering.
